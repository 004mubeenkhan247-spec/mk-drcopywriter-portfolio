The Problem

Why Most Copy Fails

The issue usually isn't
your product.
It's the gap.

There are a few patterns I see constantly in briefs, in audits, in "why isn't this converting" emails from founders who know something's off but can't pinpoint it.

I

Copy that talks about the business instead of the buyer.

"We're a passionate team of experts dedicated to excellence." I've seen some version of that on maybe 60% of the briefs that land in my inbox. It's not wrong exactly it's just not about the reader. At all. People scan for themselves first. If they don't find themselves in the first paragraph, they leave.

II

Features listed where benefits should live.

"Automated reporting dashboard." Okay so I save how much time? Avoid which headache? The feature is the mechanism. The benefit is why someone cares. Most copy stops one step too early, right before the part that would actually move someone.

III

A CTA that tells instead of invites.

"Submit." "Learn more." These read like instructions on a form, not invitations to do something worth doing. I spent an embarrassing amount of time on CTA microcopy when I was first learning turns out the button text is often the highest-leverage word on the page.

What I Write

Five things I write.
One goal: action.

I'm not a generalist who'll take any project. These are the five formats I've studied most and write best. If what you need is outside this list, I'll tell you honestly rather than wing it.

01
Email Funnels
Sequences · Campaigns · Drips
"Your list is full of real people who stopped opening your emails."

Email open rates usually don't tank because the audience lost interest. They tank because the emails became predictable same subject line pattern, same "just checking in" energy. I write sequences that give people a reason to actually open the next one.

  • Welcome & onboarding sequences
  • Nurture & re-engagement drips
  • Promotional & launch campaigns
  • Subject lines that don't look like subject lines
02
Sales Pages
Long-form · Short-form · Opt-in
"Scroll, scroll, close tab. Your page doesn't know why."

The thing most sales pages get wrong is the order. They lead with the offer before the reader has any reason to want it. I write pages that build the case first who this is for, what problem it solves, why now and let the close feel like a logical conclusion rather than a pitch.

  • Long-form sales pages
  • Short-form landing pages
  • Opt-in & lead gen pages
  • VSL scripts
03
B2B Outreach
Cold Email · LinkedIn · Follow-ups
"The reply rate on most cold email is 1–2%. The floor is not the ceiling."

Most cold email fails the same way: it's written about the sender, not about the recipient. I write outreach that leads with something specific and relevant something that makes the reader think "okay, they did their homework." That's usually the only thing standing between ignored and replied to.

  • Cold email sequences
  • LinkedIn DM copy
  • Follow-up cadences
  • Call-booking copy
04
Website Copy
Homepage · About · Services · CTA
"Read your homepage out loud. Does it sound like a person or a brochure?"

I got obsessed with website copy after reading a teardown of a homepage where the writer showed that every line was technically true but told the visitor nothing about why it mattered to them. That's where most homepage copy lives technically fine, emotionally inert. I fix that.

  • Homepage & hero section
  • About & services pages
  • Microcopy & CTA rewrite
  • Full site audits
05
Ad Copy
Facebook · Google · Retargeting
"Clicks are cheap. The wrong copy just makes them expensive."

Ad copy and landing page copy live or die together. If the ad promises one thing and the page delivers another even a slightly different tone the reader clocks the mismatch and bounces. I write both with that alignment in mind, not just the ad in isolation.

  • Facebook & Instagram ads
  • Google Search ads
  • A/B copy variants
  • Retargeting copy

Process

How It Works

Most copy fails
before a word
is written.

The brief says "write a 5-email sequence for our SaaS product." But it doesn't say who actually uses it, what they were trying before, or what they were afraid of when they signed up. So you write technically correct copy that doesn't land. I try to fix the upstream problem first.

I
Step 01

Discovery

"I'm going to ask you questions your designer never asked."

Who is the person you're writing to? What did they try before this? What are they still not sure about? What would make them feel stupid for buying? Most briefs skip all of this I need it before I can write anything useful.

II
Step 02

Research

"Your best headlines are probably hiding in your reviews."

I go through competitor copy, customer reviews, forum threads, Reddit posts anywhere your buyers describe their own problems in their own words. The goal is to find language I couldn't have invented at a desk. The writing usually comes easier once you've done this part properly.

III
Step 03

The Draft

"You'll see the reasoning, not just the result."

Every draft I deliver is annotated I explain what I was trying to do with each headline, each transition, each CTA. Not because I need to justify myself, but because it makes the revision process faster. If you disagree with my reasoning, we can fix it. If you just don't like the line, that's a different conversation.

IV
Step 04

Revisions

"I'd rather revise than deliver something that doesn't work."

I don't count rounds I revise until the copy does what it's supposed to do. If we're three drafts in and something still feels off, I'd rather figure out why than send over a technically-complete file you're not confident in.

Work Samples

The Copy & The Thinking Behind It

The copy
and the thinking.

Every sample includes what the original was, why it wasn't working, what I replaced it with, and my actual reasoning. Portfolios without the "why" don't tell you much about the writer.

Email Subject Line
SaaS productivity tool, welcome email. Original subjects: "Welcome to [Product]!" and "Your getting started guide." Open rate sitting at 19%. The founder thought it was a deliverability issue. It wasn't.

Welcome Email Subject SaaS Onboarding

You signed up. Most people stop here.
The original subjects told you what the email contained, which is fine. But "Welcome to [Product]" competes with every other welcome email in the inbox it has no reason to be opened first. This one works because it implies something without saying it. "Most people stop here" creates a tiny identity challenge the reader wants to know if they're in the group that stops, or the group that doesn't. It's not a trick. It's just more interesting than announcing yourself.
What I was thinking: The fastest way to get an open is to make the reader feel like the email is specifically about them, not about the product. This subject does that without being clever or cute about it.
SaaS / TechEmail Copy
Sales Page Headline
Business coach working with early-stage entrepreneurs. Original homepage headline: "Transform Your Business Journey." Bounce rate high, no one scrolling past the fold. The coach had a genuinely good offer. The headline just didn't communicate it.

Landing Page Headline Business Coaching

Nobody teaches you how to run a business. That's not an excuse. It's the problem I solve.
I spent a long time on this one. The original was aspirational but vague it could have belonged to a travel agency or a therapy practice. I wanted something that named the real frustration: that most people who start a business were never actually taught how. The third sentence is doing a lot it doesn't let the headline just be a complaint. It immediately turns it into a positioning statement. That's the whole funnel in three lines.
What I was thinking: Before you can sell a solution, the reader has to believe you understand the problem. This headline earns that before it earns anything else.
CoachingSales Page
Re-engagement Email
D2C skincare brand. Subscribers inactive 60+ days. Original re-engagement attempt: "We miss you! Here's 10% off." Standard. Barely moved the needle. The discount was fine the framing was the problem.

Re-engagement Email Skincare Brand

You stopped opening our emails a while back. Honestly? Fair. We were boring you.
When someone hasn't opened your emails in two months, a discount isn't what re-earns attention it just confirms you're transactional. This open works because it does something brands almost never do: admit fault. Not in a groveling way, just plainly. "We were boring you" is disarming because it's almost certainly true and almost never said. Once the reader thinks "okay, this is different," they keep reading. The offer can come after it lands better once the relationship has been reset.
What I was thinking: Re-engagement copy usually leads with what the brand wants (you back). This one leads with what actually happened (we let you down). The order matters more than the message.

B2B Cold Email
B2B software company. Sales team cold emails at 2% reply rate. Standard opener: "I'd love to show you how [Product] can help [Company] achieve its goals." The opener explained nothing and cost the reader nothing to ignore.

Cold Email Opening Line B2B SaaS

Noticed you're still running [X process] manually three of your competitors automated it last quarter. Not sure if that's a priority for you, but it took them about two weeks to switch.
This took a few drafts. The goal was to write an opener that immediately demonstrates research, creates a mild competitive itch, and removes pressure all at once. "Not sure if that's a priority for you" does that last part it takes the sales energy out of the sentence and replaces it with something almost conversational. The "two weeks" detail is deliberate: it makes the solution feel small and achievable rather than a big project. Nobody replies to cold email that feels like homework.
What I was thinking: Cold email lives or dies on whether the recipient thinks you're talking to them specifically or broadcasting at them generally. Every word here is trying to feel like the former.
B2B / SaaSOutreach Copy
CTA Microcopy Rewrite
Online course creator. Button text: "Enroll Now." High checkout abandonment. The page was actually solid good social proof, clear offer. But the button was where people stopped. I didn't believe it was about price.

Checkout CTA Rewrite Online Course

Yes, I'm ready start my first lesson today.
I've spent more time thinking about CTA microcopy than I probably should admit. "Enroll Now" is a command it tells the buyer what to do, in the brand's language. This rewrite puts the reader in the first person ("I'm ready"), which is a small but meaningful shift they're not responding to a prompt, they're making a decision. "Start my first lesson today" replaces the abstract enrollment action with the concrete thing that actually happens next. And "today" carries more urgency than "now" because it implies the outcome is immediate rather than the process.
What I was thinking: The last thing someone reads before they buy is the CTA. If it still sounds like a sales tool at that point, it can stall a decision that was already made. This version tries to sound like a conclusion, not a pitch.

I got into copywriting sideways. I wasn't looking for a career I was just one of those people who screenshots ads. Not because I liked them, but because I wanted to figure out why they worked on me even when I knew what they were doing.

That led to Gary Halbert. Then David Ogilvy. Then Eugene Schwartz, who I still think is the hardest writer to actually understand not to read, but to understand. I've read Breakthrough Advertising twice and I'm not sure I've fully got it yet. I probably re-read sections of it every few weeks.

I'm early in this career. I know that, and I don't have a polished way to dress it up. What I can say is I put a disproportionate amount of time into the research and the rewriting more than you'd expect for the rates I charge right now. Partly because I want to build a real portfolio. Partly because I find the work genuinely interesting.

Based in Karachi. Remote everywhere. I reply to emails personally and I don't use canned responses.

What I Actually Took From These Writers
Gary HalbertThe Prince of Print

The main thing Halbert taught me isn't really a technique it's an attitude. He treated every reader like a specific person who had better things to do than read his ad. So he earned attention constantly, line by line. I think about that whenever I'm writing a second paragraph that I'm not sure anyone will reach.

David OgilvyThe Father of Advertising

Ogilvy is the writer that made me realize research isn't prep work it is the work. He apparently spent weeks on a single campaign, interviewing real people, reading everything, before he wrote a word. The headline usually came last. I found that counterintuitive the first time I read it. Now it's how I work.

Eugene SchwartzBreakthrough Advertising

The awareness level concept changed how I read other people's copy. You can immediately tell when a piece of copy is pitched at the wrong awareness level it's selling too hard to someone who doesn't yet believe they have the problem, or explaining too much to someone who just wants to buy. Once you see it, you see it everywhere.
